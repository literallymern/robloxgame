    Kagundi, officially the Democratic Republic of Kagundi (German: Demokratische Republik Kagundi; Kagundisch: Demokratischi Republik Kagundi), is a landlocked country in the highlands of Africa. Its capital and largest city is Gundiville. Kagundi is formally a presidential republic, but in practice it is an authoritarian presidential dictatorship led by the Leader of the Revolution, currently Dr. Justin Wachmann.

    The territory was a German colony from 1885 and, after a four-year German military regency, a French protectorate until it negotiated its independence in 1960. A military-backed revolution on 12 May 1982 created the present order, whose institutions follow Muammar Gaddafi's Third Universal Theory in a Kagundian form, the Kagundian Way. The economy rests on gold, diamonds, palladium, timber, tobacco and beef, and Germany is the country's main trading partner.

    About 90% of Kagundians are indigenous, most of them Gundi. German and English are the official languages, but most people speak Kagundisch, a German-based creole, in daily life. Kagundi's geography is modelled on Rwanda; its history and institutions are fictional.

    Name

    The name comes from Old Gundi, the ancestral language of the indigenous population. The prefix Ka- means "land of" or "place of", so Kagundi means "land of the Gundi". Before colonial rule the region was known as the Kagundi Basin or Territory of Kagundi, and German records also call it Kagundiland. The trade city Kagundali adds the Old Gundi suffix -ali, "great", and means "great place of the Gundi".

    History

    Main articles: History of Kagundi, Timeline

    1885today

    German rule from 1885 left the country its administration, its architecture and the contact language that became Kagundisch. Cut off from Berlin in the First World War, Colonel Ernst Kessler ruled as Regent from 1915 to 1919; the brutal suppression of the 1917 Kagundali Strike became the founding story of Kagundian resistance. France governed the Kagundi Protectorate until independence on 22 November 1960. The First Republic was democratic on paper but ruled by a small elite, and it collapsed in scandal and economic crisis. The Free Officers took power on 12 May 1982 and built the revolutionary state that governs Kagundi today.

    Geography

    Main article: Geography of Kagundi

    Kagundi is landlocked and mostly mountainous, with high plateaus, forested hills, valleys and rolling farmland. The capital and all major cities lie in the East District: Gundiville in a central basin, Stokondo to the north-west, Kagundali in a south-western valley and Oberdueshuson on the north-eastern plateau. A cargo airport east of the capital and two international border crossings connect the country to the outside world.

    Government and politics

    Main articles: Government of Kagundi, Leader of the Revolution, The Kagundian Way

    The Leader of the Revolution holds supreme power. The Secretary-General of the People's Committee heads the government and its four secretariats. The General People's Congress and the local People's Councils provide formal representation through trade unions and professional organisations, but their influence is limited. Security is provided by the City Police, the Gendarmerie, the Land Forces and Air Force, the Revolutionary Guard and the Intelligence Service. Mayors judge all ordinary cases in the first instance, and appeals go to the court in Stokondo.

    Economy

    Main articles: Economy of Kagundi, Kagundi Dinar and banking, AVK

    Kagundi has a resource-based economy built on mining, forestry, agriculture and cattle, with a growing service sector of transport, construction, retail, property and finance. Private businesses work alongside state-majority-owned enterprises that date from the nationalisation of 1984. Germany supplies machinery and buys most of the country's commodities; the European Union and China are further partners.

    Society and culture

    Main articles: Demographics, Kagundisch, Culture of Kagundi

    Kagundian culture stands on German foundations with French and Old Gundi layers on top. Most people pair a German or French first name with a Gundi family name, go to Lutheran or Catholic churches while keeping ancestor customs, and value correct paperwork. Street stands sell Brati sausages, families meet for Kaffi und Kuchi on Sundays, and brass bands play Trommelblas at every festival.

    History of Kagundi

    From the Kagundi Encyclopedia

    Kagundi's history runs through six eras: the pre-colonial Kagundi Basin, German rule, a short German military regency, the French protectorate, the First Republic, and the revolutionary state since 1982. The revolutionary state has now lasted 44 years, longer than the 41-year protectorate.

    Kagundi Basin (before 1885)

    Before colonial rule, the territory was home to indigenous communities with their own regional political structures. They spoke Old Gundi, and place names with the prefix Ka- ("land of", "place of") date from this period. They remain a hallmark of Kagundian geography, as in Kagundi and Kagundali. Highland clans kept cattle and farmed the valleys, and their ancestor customs survive today in the yearly Ahnentag.

    German-Kagundi (1885–1915)

    In 1885 the territory came under German colonial rule as German-Kagundi; the name Kagundiland also appears in records and maps. The German administration shaped the country's institutions, architecture, infrastructure and commerce, and later its political elite. German settler communities took root.

    • The colonial capital, Gundistadt, was founded on the site of today's Gundiville.
    • Lutheran and Catholic missions opened the first schools. Schools, plantations and mines used a simplified German to communicate with the many local communities. The children of these workers grew up with it as their first language, and over generations it became Kagundisch.
    • Gold mining began in the hills around today's Kagundali, and tobacco plantations spread across the Oberdueshuson highlands.
    • The colonial defence force, the Schutztruppe, built its main garrison at Oberdueshuson. The town has been the country's military centre ever since.

    German Regency (1915–1919)

    The First World War cut German-Kagundi off from Berlin: no ships, mail, money or orders arrived. In 1915 the commander of the Schutztruppe, Colonel Ernst Kessler, removed the civilian colonial administration and proclaimed himself Landesverweser (Regent). He claimed to hold the territory in trust for Germany but ruled on his own authority. Kagundians remember these years as the Verweserzeit, the Regency.

    • 1915: Kessler declared martial law, and German settlers formed an armed militia to support the regime.
    • 1916: To pay his troops, the regime forced indigenous men to work in the Kagundali gold mines and requisitioned cattle and tobacco.
    • 1917: The miners of Kagundali went on strike and the Schutztruppe broke the strike by force. The Kagundali Strike became the founding story of Kagundian resistance, and the strikers are honoured as national heroes on Streiktag, 8 June.
    • 1918: The war ended in Europe. The regime lost its last claim to legitimacy, and food shortages spread.
    • 1919: Germany gave up Kagundi in the peace settlement. French troops entered Gundistadt; Kessler surrendered without a fight and was deported to Europe.

    The Regency left a lasting precedent: when civilian government fails, the military takes power. The officers of 1982 followed the same pattern, but they justified it in the name of the 1917 strikers, not the German regent.

    Kagundi Protectorate (1919–1960)

    France governed the territory as the Kagundi Protectorate. French influence became a second layer in the country's institutions and culture, and many families gained mixed German and French ancestry.

    • 1920: Gundistadt was renamed Gundiville.
    • 1923: The French administration founded the Gendarmerie du Kagundi, forerunner of today's Gendarmerie.
    • French words such as mersi (thanks), biro (office) and schandarm (gendarme) entered Kagundisch.
    • 1950s: Teachers, trade unionists and mission-school graduates founded the Kagundi National Union (Kagundischer Nationalbund), the movement that negotiated independence.

    Independence and the First Republic (1960–1982)

    Independence was proclaimed in Gundiville on 22 November 1960, and the new state took the name Democratic Republic of Kagundi. Its first constitution combined a French-style presidency with a German-style civil service. The First Republic was democratic on paper, but power stayed with a small elite in Gundiville: German- and French-descended families, senior civil servants and the leadership of the National Union. Foreign companies kept most mining concessions, so most of the profit from gold and diamonds left the country.

    • 1960–1969: President Friedrich Kalembe, leader of the independence movement, governed. His "national unity" policy kept the colonial civil service and officer corps in place.
    • 1964: The government recruited ranch managers, veterinarians and mining engineers from Argentina and Uruguay to modernise cattle farming and the mines. This is the origin of today's Argentinian and Uruguayan communities.
    • 1969: Kalembe died in office. Henri Ondaru won the 1970 presidential election amid widespread accusations of fraud.
    • 1973: Large palladium deposits were found near Kagundali. Ondaru sold the mining rights to a foreign consortium far below their value.
    • 1977: Army officers leaked the palladium contract. The Concession Scandal destroyed the government's reputation.
    • 1979–1981: Gold and commodity prices fell and the state ran out of money. The old currency, the Kagundi Franc, lost much of its value, and soldiers and teachers went unpaid for months.
    • 1981: Miners in Kagundali and tobacco workers around Oberdueshuson went on strike. Ondaru ordered the army to end the strikes by force, and several units refused.

    Revolution of 1982

    Main article: Revolution of 1982

    On the night of 11 to 12 May 1982, the Movement of Free Officers marched from the Oberdueshuson garrison to Gundiville and took the radio station, the airport and the presidential palace. President Ondaru fled abroad and the takeover was almost bloodless. Their leader, Major Wachmann, announced the revolution on national radio and took the title Leader of the Revolution. The state kept its name, Democratic Republic of Kagundi.

    The revolutionary state (1982–today)

    • 1982–1983: A Revolutionary Command Council of the Free Officers governed, then handed formal power to the new system of People's Councils, the General People's Congress and the People's Committee.
    • 1983: The government adopted a Kagundian version of Gaddafi's Third Universal Theory as its state doctrine. The Leader visited Tripoli, and Libyan advisers helped build the congress system.
    • 1983: The Kagundi Dinar, named after the Libyan dinar, replaced the Kagundi Franc, and the officers who led the revolution formed the core of the new Revolutionary Guard.
    • 1984: Foreign mining concessions were nationalised and turned into state-majority-owned enterprises. The Leader published Der Kagundische Weg ("The Kagundian Way").
    • Late 1980s: Despite its revolutionary language, the government signed machinery and trade agreements with West Germany. Germany became, and remains, Kagundi's main trading partner.
    • 2000s: Trade with the European Union and China grew.
    • 2021: The founding Leader died. The Revolutionary Guard proclaimed his son, Dr. Justin Wachmann, as the new Leader of the Revolution, and the General People's Congress confirmed him by acclamation.
    • Today: Dr. Justin Wachmann leads the revolutionary state. Ro-Nation is set in the present day.

    The Revolution of 1982 was the military-backed takeover in which the Movement of Free Officers overthrew President Henri Ondaru and founded the revolutionary state that governs Kagundi today. It is the source of the office of the Leader of the Revolution and of the state's legitimacy.

    Background

    The First Republic had promised democracy after independence in 1960, but power stayed with a small elite and foreign companies kept the mines. Three developments turned discontent into revolt:

    • The Concession Scandal (1977). In 1973 President Ondaru had sold the rights to newly found palladium deposits near Kagundali to a foreign consortium far below their value. When army officers leaked the contract in 1977, the government lost what was left of its reputation.
    • Economic collapse (1979–1981). Gold and commodity prices fell, the treasury ran dry and the Kagundi Franc lost much of its value. Soldiers and teachers went unpaid for months.
    • The strike order (1981). When miners in Kagundali and tobacco workers around Oberdueshuson struck, Ondaru ordered the army to end the strikes by force. Several units refused. To many officers the order recalled 1917, when the Schutztruppe had crushed the Kagundali Strike.

    The night of 11 to 12 May

    A group of junior and mid-ranking officers, most of them from the Oberdueshuson garrison, had organised themselves as the Movement of Free Officers. On the evening of 11 May 1982 their units left the garrison and drove down from the plateau towards the capital. Before dawn they occupied the national radio station, the airport and the presidential palace in Gundiville. Most of the army stayed in its barracks or joined them. President Ondaru fled abroad, and the takeover was almost bloodless.

    In the morning the leader of the officers, the 29-year-old Major Wachmann from a German-descended officer family, announced the revolution on national radio. From that day on he was known by his title, Leader of the Revolution.

    The four reasons

    In the radio announcement the officers named four reasons for the revolution. They are still taught in schools:

    1. The corruption of the Ondaru government.
    2. Foreign control of the mines.
    3. Unpaid wages.
    4. The order to fire on striking workers.

    Revolutionary Command Council

    From May 1982 a Revolutionary Command Council of the Free Officers governed the country. It kept the name Democratic Republic of Kagundi and, in 1983, handed formal power to a new system modelled on Libya: local People's Councils, the General People's Congress and the People's Committee with its Secretary-General and secretariats. The Leader kept supreme authority as the one who "guides" the revolution.

    Aftermath

    • 1983: adoption of a Kagundian version of Gaddafi's Third Universal Theory as state doctrine; the Leader's visit to Tripoli; introduction of the Kagundi Dinar; formation of the Revolutionary Guard around the Free Officers.
    • 1984: nationalisation of the foreign mining concessions, which became state-majority-owned enterprises, and publication of Der Kagundische Weg.
    • Late 1980s: a pragmatic turn towards West Germany, which became the main trading partner.

    Legacy

    12 May is the national day, Revolution Day, marked by a military parade in Gundiville. The rayed yellow sun on the flag stands for the dawn of 12 May 1982. Many families of the Free Officers still hold high office, and the Revolutionary Guard sees itself as the protector of the revolution. The precedent of 1915 and 1982, that the military steps in when civilian government fails, remains part of the country's political memory.

    Notable Kagundians

    From the Kagundi Encyclopedia

    Short biographies of the people who shaped Kagundi's history, in order of their time in power.

    Colonel Ernst Kessler

    Regent of Kagundi, 1915–1919. Commander of the German Schutztruppe in German-Kagundi. When the First World War cut the colony off from Berlin, Kessler removed the civilian administration in 1915 and made himself Landesverweser. His regime used forced labour in the Kagundali gold mines and broke the 1917 Kagundali Strike by force. In 1919 he surrendered to French troops without a fight and was deported to Europe. In Kagundi his name stands for the Verweserzeit, the years of military rule in Germany's name.

    Friedrich Kalembe

    First President of Kagundi, 1960–1969. Leader of the Kagundi National Union, the movement of teachers, trade unionists and mission-school graduates that negotiated independence. As President he followed a policy of "national unity" that kept the colonial civil service and officer corps in place, and in 1964 he recruited skilled workers from Argentina and Uruguay. He died in office in 1969. His name is a typical Kagundian pairing of a German first name with a Gundi family name.

    Henri Ondaru

    President of Kagundi, 1970–1982. Won the 1970 presidential election amid widespread accusations of fraud. In 1973 he sold the rights to the palladium deposits near Kagundali far below their value, which led to the Concession Scandal of 1977. During the economic crisis of 1979–1981 he ordered the army to break the strikes in Kagundali and Oberdueshuson. When the Free Officers took Gundiville on 12 May 1982, he fled abroad.

    Major Wachmann

    Founding Leader of the Revolution, 1982–2021. Born about 1953 into a German-descended officer family, he served at the Oberdueshuson garrison and led the Movement of Free Officers. At 29 he announced the revolution on national radio on 12 May 1982. He chaired the Revolutionary Command Council, adopted the Third Universal Theory in 1983, visited Tripoli, created the Kagundi Dinar and the Revolutionary Guard, and published Der Kagundische Weg in 1984. Despite his revolutionary language he made West Germany the country's main trading partner. He died in 2021 after 39 years in power. State media still call him "the founding Leader"; the title of Major he kept for life.

    Dr. Justin Wachmann

    Leader of the Revolution since 2021. Son of the founding Leader and, like his father, a German-descended Kagundian. He studied law and medicine at the national university in Gundiville, served as a general in the Land Forces and in the Revolutionary Guard protecting his father, and was his father's personal physician. After his father's death in 2021 the Revolutionary Guard proclaimed him Leader of the Revolution, and the General People's Congress confirmed him by acclamation. His residence is in Gundiville, and a large statue of him stands on the main square facing the General People's Congress. Officially he is addressed and quoted by his title.

    Geography of Kagundi

    From the Kagundi Encyclopedia

    Kagundi is a landlocked highland country of mountains, high plateaus, forested hills, valleys and farmland. The capital and all major cities lie in the East District, which is ringed by mountains and connected to its neighbours by two border crossings and a cargo airport.

    Landscape

    A ring of steep mountains encloses the East District. Inside it the land falls from the northern highlands and the north-eastern plateau, where Oberdueshuson stands, down to the central basin of Gundiville and the south-western valley of Kagundali. Forested hills separate the capital from Stokondo, and rolling lowland pastures fill the south-east. Mines and industrial sites lie outside the commercial centres, while tobacco farms and cattle ranches fill the rural districts.

    Climate

    Like the African highlands it is modelled on, Kagundi has a mild climate that changes little through the year. Altitude matters more than season: the plateau around Oberdueshuson is the coolest part of the district, the valley of Kagundali the warmest. Two rainy seasons, in spring and in late autumn, keep the hills green.

    Transport

    Without a coastline, Kagundi trades by air cargo and through its border crossings, so road freight connects the farms, forests and mines to the trading centres. The road network is built as a hub and a ring:

    • Main roads run from Gundiville to each of the other three cities, to the cargo airport and the east border, and to the south border.
    • Regional roads form an outer ring: through the western valley from Stokondo to Kagundali past the palladium mine; over the northern pass from Stokondo to Oberdueshuson, the highest road in the country; from Kagundali to the southern main road, the freight route to the south border; and a military road from Oberdueshuson past the training grounds to the airport road.
    • Every city can be reached by at least two routes, which matters for emergency services and for roadblocks.

    A river runs from the northern highlands south past the eastern edge of Gundiville, crossed by several bridges.

    Natural resources

    • Gold and diamonds: the hills south-west of Kagundali, mined since the German period.
    • Palladium: the western hills near Kagundali, discovered in 1973.
    • Timber: the forested hills between Gundiville and Stokondo.
    • Tobacco: the highlands around Oberdueshuson.
    • Cattle: the south-eastern lowland pastures.

    Infrastructure

    The East District has fuel stations in every city and along the main roads, vehicle workshops in Gundiville, Kagundali and Oberdueshuson, water and electricity networks, telecommunications, waste management, schools and vocational training. Bus stops line the main roads at city entrances and villages.

    Gundiville is the capital and largest city of Kagundi and its political, administrative and commercial heart. All main roads of the East District meet here.

    History

    The German colonial administration founded the city as Gundistadt. The French protectorate renamed it Gundiville in 1920. Independence was proclaimed here on 22 November 1960, and on the night of 11 to 12 May 1982 the Free Officers took its radio station, airport and presidential palace. Every 12 May the Revolution Day parade marches up the ceremonial avenue to the main square.

    Government district

    The government district lies around the main square, a ceremonial and market square with market stalls along its east and west edges and space for parades in the middle. A tree-lined ceremonial avenue leads to it from the south.

    Landmarks

    • General People's Congress: the tallest building of the district, with a central dome, closing the north end of the main square. Ceremonial steps lead down to the forecourt.
    • Statue of Dr. Justin Wachmann: a large, permanent statue of the Leader of the Revolution on the ceremonial axis, directly in front of the Congress and facing it. It can be seen along the whole avenue.
    • Hausali: the government headquarters (Kagundisch for "great house"), an administrative building on the east side of the square, seat of the Secretary-General and the secretariats.
    • Leader's residence: a walled, guarded compound with gardens north-east of the square, set apart from the Congress and the Hausali and protected by the Revolutionary Guard.
    • Central Bank of Kagundi: on the west side of the square.
    • National university: a campus in the north-west with the country's only law programme, which grants the justice degree required of lawyers and prosecutors.
    • City Police headquarters: the main police station, on the avenue south of the square, with a vehicle yard behind it.
    • Central hospital: east of the avenue, with an ambulance bay and a helipad.
    • Brandwehr Crisis Response Center: on the main east–west street, its apparatus bays opening towards the airport road.
    • Commercial district and Market Street: shops, offices and a market hall along the avenue.
    • Bus terminal: at the south end of the avenue, where most visitors arrive.

    Kagundali

    From the Kagundi Encyclopedia

    Kagundali"great place of the Gundi"
    RoleTrade, freight and mining
    LocationLowland valley south-west of Gundiville
    Known forGold, diamonds and palladium; 1917 Kagundali Strike
    RoadsMain road to Gundiville; regional roads to Stokondo and to the south border

    Kagundali is the commercial and mining centre of Kagundi, an energetic town at the foot of rugged green hills. Its name combines Ka-, Gundi and the Old Gundi suffix -ali, "great".

    History

    Gold mining began in the hills around Kagundali under German rule. During the Regency the regime forced indigenous men to work in the mines, and in 1917 the miners went on strike. The Schutztruppe broke the Kagundali Strike by force; it became the founding story of Kagundian resistance, and a memorial in the town honours the strikers. In 1973 palladium was found in the western hills, and the sale of the rights far below their value led to the Concession Scandal of 1977. The miners' strike of 1981 and the order to fire on them were among the causes of the Revolution of 1982.

    Economy

    • Mining: gold fields and diamond workings in the hills to the south-west, and the palladium mine in the western hills, all with their own haul roads.
    • Ore processing: an industrial zone between the town and the capital road.
    • Trade and freight: trading companies, warehouses, loading bays and a freight yard on the east side of town. Freight leaves by the main road to Gundiville and the airport, or by the regional road to the south border.
    • Workshops and workforce housing around the commercial core.

    Government of Kagundi

    From the Kagundi Encyclopedia

    Kagundi is a centralised unitary state. Formally it is a presidential republic; in practice it is an authoritarian presidential dictatorship in which power is concentrated in the Leader of the Revolution. A system of people's councils and congresses, modelled on Libya under Muammar Gaddafi, provides formal representation.

    Secretary-General of the People's Committee

    The Secretary-General is the head of government and directs the administration. The Leader appoints the Secretary-General, and the General People's Congress confirms the appointment. The Secretary-General can give instructions to every secretariat, in coordination with the Leader, and decides whether women are called up for military service in wartime. Secretaries are appointed and dismissed jointly by the Secretary-General and the Leader.

    The four secretariats

    General People's Congress

    The General People's Congress is the national representative body. Its members come directly from the People's Councils. It reviews and discusses the budget, confirms designated officials, submits proposals and motions, and formally confirms government decisions. Its real influence is far smaller than the Leader's.

    People's Councils

    People's Councils work at the local level. They give advice and proposals, run limited municipal self-government and take part in appointing the mayors of towns and villages. Their members are chosen through trade unions and professional organisations. Criticism and opposing motions are allowed on paper but strongly restricted in practice.

    Parties and civic organisations

    Political parties are not expressly banned, but independent political organisations cannot operate freely. Trade unions and professional associations are directed by the state and are the main formal channels for representing workers and professions. The result is limited participation without real competition between parties.

    Administration

    The country is divided into districts; the East District contains the capital and all major cities. Towns and villages are run by mayors appointed through the People's Councils, while the national government keeps the main political and administrative powers. Laws and government decisions are published in the official government gazette and through state announcements. Paperwork matters: officials are addressed by title, as in Herr Sekretär or Frau Maista, and nothing happens without a stamp.

    The Leader of the Revolution is the highest authority in Kagundi. The office combines the functions of a president with nearly unrestricted power over the government and the security institutions.

    A title, not an office

    "Leader of the Revolution" is the holder's title, comparable to Gaddafi's title in Libya, "Brotherly Leader and Guide of the Revolution". It comes from leading the 1982 revolution, not from an election, so it has no term, no election and no age limit. In German it is Leiter der Revolution, in Kagundisch di Laita; the German word Führer is never used.

    Officially the Leader is addressed and quoted by the title. The personal name is publicly known, but state media rarely use it.

    Powers

    • Sets the political direction of the country and holds supreme executive authority.
    • Appoints the Secretary-General of the People's Committee, and with the Secretary-General appoints and dismisses the secretaries.
    • Commands the Revolutionary Guard directly, and through it the Intelligence Service.
    • Has direct influence over the highest government and security institutions.

    Official doctrine says the Leader only "guides" the revolution while the people govern themselves through their councils and congress. In practice the Leader holds the powers above.

    The Kagundian Way

    From the Kagundi Encyclopedia

    The Kagundian Way (Der Kagundische Weg) is the state doctrine of Kagundi since 1983: a selective, Kagundian version of Muammar Gaddafi's Third Universal Theory.

    Origin

    Gaddafi set out the Third Universal Theory in his Green Book (1975–1979) as a third way between capitalism and communism, in which the people rule directly through congresses and committees instead of parliaments and parties. After the revolution the Free Officers looked to Libya for a model. In 1983 the Leader visited Tripoli and Libyan advisers helped build the congress system. In 1984 the Leader published the booklet Der Kagundische Weg, which explains which ideas apply to Kagundi.

    What Kagundi took from the Green Book

    Idea from the Green BookIn Kagundi
    "Representation is fraud": no parliamentThe General People's Congress and People's Councils replace a parliament
    Parties divide the peopleParties are not banned but cannot operate freely
    Committees govern instead of ministersThe People's Committee, its Secretary-General and the secretariats; the names follow Libya
    Workers and professions take part through their organisationsCouncil members are chosen through trade unions and professional organisations
    "Partners, not wage-workers"A slogan on posters only; wages, rent and private businesses stay legal because Kagundi depends on German trade and investment
    The Leader guides, the people ruleThe official doctrine of the Leader's role

    In daily life

    The Green Book is taught in schools in a German translation, and its slogans appear on posters and public buildings. The doctrine shapes the institutions and the language of the state, while the economy stays pragmatic and closely tied to Germany. The revolution's four reasons, from corruption to the order to fire on strikers, are part of every school curriculum.

    Who handles a case

    The seriousness of an offence decides which force takes the lead:

    1. Ordinary offenceCity Police: theft, traffic, public order, assault.
    2. Serious crime (Verbrechen)Gendarmerie takes the lead once involved, under the German-law-inspired classification.
    3. Threat to the nationIntelligence Service: terrorism, mass murder, destruction of critical infrastructure.
    4. Misconduct by officersRevolutionary Guard investigates the security services themselves.

    The Verbrechen category refers to the legal seriousness of an offence, not to the suspect's origin or nationality.

    City Police

    The City Police are Kagundi's everyday police. They patrol, enforce traffic rules, keep public order, make arrests, secure evidence and investigate ordinary crime. They prepare the case files, and an officer with a justice degree prosecutes their cases in court. The force has its own command structure under the Secretariat for Internal Affairs and Infrastructure; its headquarters is the main police station on the ceremonial avenue in Gundiville.

    Gendarmerie

    The Gendarmerie is a militarily organised national police force. It belongs to the armed forces, is overseen by the Secretariat for Defence and Foreign Affairs and has full police authority. It is more heavily organised than the City Police and takes the lead in serious crimes and major security incidents. It traces its origin to the Gendarmerie du Kagundi, founded by the French protectorate in 1923.

    Land Forces and Air Force

    The armed forces are responsible for national defence and military training. All men serve two years of basic military service without ordinary exemptions and remain registered as reservists for life. In wartime the Secretary-General decides whether women are also called up.

    Revolutionary Guard

    The Revolutionary Guard is a special military unit directly under the Leader of the Revolution. It was formed in 1983 around the Free Officers and the units that marched on Gundiville in May 1982, and it sees itself as the protector of the revolution. It protects the Leader and the Secretary-General, guards designated state facilities, carries out state-security tasks and investigates misconduct within the security services. Its members sit as judges in the Military Tribunal. Its main barracks, parade ground and training grounds are in Oberdueshuson.

    Intelligence Service

    The Intelligence Service is subordinate to the Revolutionary Guard. It handles the most serious cases: terrorism, mass murder, major destruction of critical infrastructure and serious threats to national security. Ordinary offences stay with the police unless their seriousness requires the Service.

    Justice system of Kagundi

    From the Kagundi Encyclopedia

    The justice system is run by the Secretariat for Internal Affairs and Infrastructure. It has two instances, plus a separate Military Tribunal. A defining feature is that town mayors, sitting as Maista, judge every ordinary case in the first instance.

    How a case goes

    1. InvestigationThe responsible police force secures evidence and prepares the case file.
    2. ChargeAn officer of that force with a justice degree brings the prosecution.
    3. First instanceThe mayor of the town where the offence happened hears the case and gives judgment with a written reason.
    4. AppealEither side may appeal within 72 hours.
    5. Second instanceThe appeals court in Stokondo decides.
    6. Military TribunalMilitary offences and state-security crimes go here instead, with Revolutionary Guard judges.

    First instance: the mayors

    Mayors judge all ordinary cases in the first instance, from minor offences and civil disputes to serious crimes. A mayor sitting as judge is addressed as Maista. Rules of procedure:

    • Which mayor: the mayor of the town where the offence took place; in civil disputes, the mayor of the town where the defendant lives or where the business is registered.
    • Conflict of interest: a mayor may not judge a case in which they are a party, a witness, a relative, or an employer or business partner of a party. The Secretariat for Internal Affairs and Infrastructure then gives the case to another town's mayor.
    • No degree needed: mayors need no justice degree to judge, but they are bound by the published legal code.
    • Written record: every judgment is recorded with a short written reason in the judicial archive in Stokondo.

    Second instance: Stokondo

    The second instance hears appeals. Appeals must be filed within 72 hours of the first-instance judgment. Its judges are appointed by the responsible secretary. There is no career of professional judges, but a justice degree is expected and preferred for appointment. The court sits in Stokondo.

    Military Tribunal

    The Military Tribunal handles military offences and state-security crimes. Members of the Revolutionary Guard serve as judges, and only in this tribunal.

    Lawyers and prosecutors

    Defendants are assigned lawyers with a justice degree. The prosecution is brought by representatives of the police authority responsible for the case, who also need a justice degree. The degree is earned through the law programme of the national university in Gundiville. Because prosecutors come from the police, the City Police and the Gendarmerie each need at least one officer with a justice degree to bring cases to court.

    Fines

    The City Police, the Gendarmerie and judges can issue fines. A fine must be paid within three days and can be up to 50,000 KGD. Fines can be appealed like any other decision, and a judge can cancel a fine.

    Kagundi Dinar and banking

    From the Kagundi Encyclopedia

    The Kagundi Dinar (KGD) is the currency of Kagundi. It replaced the Kagundi Franc in 1983, and its name follows the Libyan dinar as a sign of the revolutionary government's closeness to Libya.

    History

    Under the protectorate and the First Republic, Kagundi used the Kagundi Franc. The economic crisis of 1979–1981 destroyed much of its value and left soldiers and teachers unpaid, one of the causes of the revolution. In 1983 the revolutionary government introduced the Dinar.

    Central Bank of Kagundi

    The Central Bank works under the Secretariat for Economy and Education and is housed on the main square of Gundiville. It sets the policy rate, which was 6% at launch and can range from 0 to 25%. A higher rate makes loans more expensive and strengthens the Dinar. The exchange rate floats: it moves by at most 0.5% a day, weakening when the money supply grows quickly and strengthening with a high policy rate and a trade surplus. In an emergency the Central Bank can finance the treasury directly, which increases the money supply and inflation.

    National Treasury

    The National Treasury receives income tax, turnover and distribution tax, export duties and import tariffs, property and transfer taxes, registration and licence fees, fines, rents and sales of state property, and dividends from state-majority-owned companies. It pays public wages and spends on eleven budget lines: government and administration; police and Gendarmerie; defence and Revolutionary Guard; justice; healthcare and EMS; Brandwehr and civil protection; education; infrastructure; public services and AVK; public procurement; and other expenditure. Any single payment above 25,000 KGD needs the approval of a second official.

    Banking

    Every citizen has a bank account for wages, transfers and payments. Businesses have their own business accounts. Banks offer transfers, credit cards and personal loans, and invoices can be sent between citizens, businesses and the state. New citizens receive a one-time starting grant of 2,500 KGD.

    A claim can be made once a policy has been active for at least two days. AVK officers review each claim and accept or reject it. A policy lapses after three days of unpaid premiums. Private health insurance is also allowed for extra benefits and private treatment.

    Health and emergency services

    From the Kagundi Encyclopedia

    Kagundi has a tax-funded, single-payer public health system, supplemented by private clinics and private insurance. The Secretariat for Health and Crisis Management oversees healthcare, emergency medical services and the Brandwehr.

    Hospitals and clinics

    • The central hospital in Gundiville, with an ambulance bay and a helipad.
    • Polyclinics in the major cities, most importantly in Stokondo.
    • Smaller clinics in smaller towns and specialised private clinics.
    • Regional emergency coordination.

    Emergency Medical Services

    EMS responds to medical emergencies and works with the hospitals, polyclinics, City Police, Gendarmerie and Brandwehr. Public and private providers take part, coordinated by region.

    Brandwehr

    The Brandwehr is the Fire and Civil Protection Service. Its firefighters, called Brandi in Kagundisch, handle building and industrial fires, road accidents, technical rescue, major industrial incidents, evacuations and public-safety emergencies, and they coordinate large-scale crises. The Crisis Response Center in Gundiville directs major emergencies together with EMS, the police and the Gendarmerie through shared emergency communications.

    Insurance

    The state insurer AVK offers supplementary health cover for private treatment as well as vehicle, property, business, liability and accident insurance.

    Demographics of Kagundi

    From the Kagundi Encyclopedia

    About nine in ten Kagundians are indigenous, most of them Gundi. German, Argentinian, French and Uruguayan communities make up the rest.

    Population groups

    Indigenous (mainly Gundi)90% German5% Argentinian2.5% French1.5% Uruguayan1%

    Most indigenous Kagundians call themselves Gundi. Smaller highland communities keep their own clan names and traditions, but all share the Kagundisch language. Many Kagundians have mixed German and French ancestry. German-descended families have lived in the country since the colonial era and are over-represented in political office, partly because many Free Officers of 1982 came from German-descended officer families. The Argentinian and Uruguayan communities grew out of the recruitment of ranch managers, veterinarians and mining engineers from 1964.

    Ancestry does not determine a person's citizenship, language, profession or politics.

    Citizenship

    Kagundi does not allow dual citizenship. Citizenship and naturalisation are handled by the country's legal institutions according to the law.

    Languages

    German and English are the official languages: German is the language of the state and its institutions, English the language of international contact. In everyday life most people speak Kagundisch, a German-based creole. It is not an official language, but it is the mother tongue of the indigenous majority and understood by everyone. Old Gundi survives only in songs, blessings and ceremonies.

    Religion

    Most Kagundians are Christian: Lutheran in the former German mission areas and Catholic in the former French ones. Many families also keep Old Gundi ancestor customs. The revolutionary state favours no church.

    Kagundisch (English Kagundish, natively Gundisch) is the everyday language of Kagundi, a creole that grew from the simplified German used between Germans and local communities after 1885.

    Origin

    Mission schools, plantations and mines used a simplified German as the common language between the colonists and the many local communities. The workers' children grew up with it as their first language, and it became a full creole language, much like Unserdeutsch in Papua New Guinea. Its words come from three layers: German (about nine in ten), French from the protectorate (mostly for administration and courtesy), and Old Gundi (place names, family names, ceremonies and a few grammar elements such as Ka-, ma- and -ali).

    Culture of Kagundi

    From the Kagundi Encyclopedia

    Kagundian culture stands on German foundations, with French and Old Gundi layers on top.

    Names

    Most Kagundians pair a German or French first name with a Gundi family name, such as Friedrich Kalembe, Marie-Luise Ondaru, Johann Masengi or Claudette Tumbalo. Gundi family names usually end in a vowel. Settler families keep German or French surnames, and Argentinian and Uruguayan families keep Spanish ones.

    Religion

    Most people are Christian, Lutheran in the former German mission areas and Catholic in the former French ones. Many families also keep Old Gundi ancestor customs, above all on Ahnentag. The revolutionary state favours no church.

    Food and drink

    • Brati: a grilled sausage of Kagundi beef, sold at street stands in every town.
    • Maisknedel: maize dumplings, served with bean stew and fried plantain.
    • Kaffi und Kuchi: strong, sweet highland coffee with cake, the Sunday afternoon family tradition.

    Music

    Trommelblas mixes colonial brass-band marches with Gundi drums. It is played at weddings, festivals and state parades, above all on Revolution Day.

    Customs

    Punctuality and correct paperwork count as civic virtues. The saying "Ohne Stempel nix" ("nothing without a stamp") is common, and officials are addressed by title, as in Herr Sekretär or Frau Maista. A stranger is politely addressed as Birger, citizen.

    Education

    Education falls under the Secretariat for Economy and Education. Schools teach in German, and the Green Book is taught in German translation. Vocational training prepares workers for the mines, farms and trades. The national university in Gundiville runs the country's law programme, which grants the justice degree required of lawyers and prosecutors.

    National symbols of Kagundi

    From the Kagundi Encyclopedia

    Flag

    The national flag was introduced after the 1982 revolution. It has a black triangle at the hoist, edged in white. The rest of the flag is divided horizontally into a blue upper band, a narrow red stripe above a yellow stripe, and a green lower band. Inside the triangle, a rayed yellow sun surrounds a white disc with two zigzag lines, black above red.

    What the flag means

    1. Black triangle: the Kagundian people.
    2. White edge: peace and unity between Kagundi's communities.
    3. Yellow sun: the dawn of 12 May 1982 and the revolution.
    4. Zigzag lines: an Old Gundi pattern for the mountains and rivers of the highlands.
    5. Blue: the sky over the highlands.
    6. Red: the blood of the 1917 Kagundali strikers and of the revolution.
    7. Yellow: gold and the country's mineral wealth.
    8. Green: forests, farmland and pastures.

    Emblem

    The national emblem places the flag's design in a circle inside a gold ring. It appears as a seal on government documents, uniforms, vehicles and public buildings.

    Other symbols

    • National day: Revolution Day, 12 May.
    • Seat of power: the Hausali and the General People's Congress on the main square of Gundiville, with the statue of the Leader between them.
    • Heroes: the strikers of Kagundali (1917) and the Free Officers (1982).
